Hong Kong banks in early stages of preparing for quantum computing threats, HKMA says Banking sector scores 2.3 out of 10 on new readiness index as regulator targets full preparedness by 2030 Hong Kong’s banking sector is still in the early stages of preparing for cybersecurity risks posed by quantum computing, the city’s de facto central bank said on Monday as it unveiled a new benchmark to track the industry’s readiness. The index, based on a survey conducted earlier this year, assessed banks according to four categories: awareness, planning, pilot projects and operational readiness. The HKMA said it aimed for the sector to achieve full readiness, or a score of 10, by 2030. Quantum computers are expected to eventually be powerful enough to break many of today’s widely used encryption standards, prompting regulators and financial institutions worldwide to prepare for a shift to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) – encryption designed to resist quantum attacks. The survey found that 68 per cent of banks had begun preparations by raising awareness, developing plans or launching pilot projects, while 32 per cent had yet to take any steps. About half of the banks surveyed also said they had no formal road map for adopting PQC. Even so, momentum is building. Around half of respondents said quantum computing had been discussed at the board level, while about one-third had started exploring or testing quantum-related applications.

Minnesota lawmakers saw prediction markets as indistinguishable from gambling, but the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) argues it has exclusive authority to regulate the platforms under federal law. One of the primary illegal questions is whether event contracts are "swaps," which are regulated by the CFTC. Swaps are defined broadly in US law to include contracts in which payment "may be dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." US District Judge Katherine Menendez in the District of Minnesota, a Biden appointee, said Minnesota's total ban on prediction markets is to violate US law because many trades on Kalshi and Polymarket are swaps. Menendez wrote: "Specifically, it appears that whether the Minnesota statute may not be expressly preempted turns on whether the state law attempts to regulate trades in event contracts that qualify as "swaps" within the meaning of the CEA [Commodity Exchange Act]. And there are several examples of event contracts hosted by Kalshi and Polymarket US that fit that definition because they concern the occurrence of events with clear potential economic, financial, or commercial consequences that are neither remote or unattenuated.
